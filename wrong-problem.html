Issue 08 · July 2026

The most expensive mistake isn’t a bad decision. It’s solving the wrong problem.

When results stall, teams often move quickly. They change the agency, increase the budget, refresh the website or launch another campaign. Movement feels productive. But if the diagnosis is wrong, it only makes the wrong answer more expensive.

The problem you see is rarely the problem you need to solve

“We need more leads” may actually mean the offer is unclear. “The website is not converting” may mean the traffic is irrelevant. “Sales is not following up” may mean marketing and sales never agreed on what a qualified lead looks like.

These are not small differences. Each one demands a different decision, owner, budget and measure of success. Treating all of them as a channel problem is how businesses spend months improving activity without improving outcomes.

A practical 4D framework

01

Define

State the problem in business terms, not channel language. What outcome is missing, for whom, and compared with what?

02

Diagnose

Look for evidence before choosing a solution. Review the buyer journey, message, traffic quality, sales response and conversion points.

03

Decide

Choose the smallest intervention that addresses the real constraint. Be clear about what you are deliberately not doing yet.

04

Design

Turn the decision into an operating plan: owner, timeline, budget, leading indicators and the point at which you will review it.

What this looks like in practice

Imagine a B2B firm receiving 200 leads every month while sales says the leads are poor. The easy response is to change targeting or buy more data. A proper diagnosis asks harder questions: Does the campaign promise the same thing that sales can deliver? Does the form filter for company size, need or decision authority? Are leads contacted quickly? Is the sales team recording why a lead was rejected?

Without those answers, “better leads” is an opinion. With them, the team can identify whether the constraint is the market, the message, the qualification process or the follow-up system.

Slow down before you speed up

Good marketing is not just the ability to execute quickly. It is the discipline to ask whether the work being requested will change the business outcome that matters. The next time a result disappoints, resist the urge to prescribe the channel first.

Define the outcome. Diagnose the constraint. Decide what matters. Then design the work.
